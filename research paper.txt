## Abstract

Choosing a playing XI from a squad is a multi-objective decision: a selector wants strong batting and strong bowling from the same eleven players. Ahmed, Deb and Jindal [1] showed that this can be treated as a bi-objective optimisation problem solved with NSGA-II, but their player scores were static career averages and their evidence was a theoretical comparison with one IPL team. We propose a *predict-then-optimise* pipeline that (i) builds leakage-free, recency-aware features for 5,108 players from 4,801 ball-by-ball T20 matches (3,558 international, 1,243 IPL), (ii) predicts each player's next-match runs and wickets with Ridge regression, XGBoost and a feed-forward neural network, (iii) converts the predictions into team batting and bowling strength, and (iv) solves the resulting constrained bi-objective problem with NSGA-II, choosing a final XI at the knee of the Pareto front. On a strict time split (test: 2025 onwards) XGBoost and the neural network reduce the mean absolute error of predicted runs from 14.42 (rolling-form baseline) to 13.78 and 13.81, and the match-level bootstrap confidence intervals of the improvement exclude zero; the two models are statistically indistinguishable from each other and from Ridge. NSGA-II recovers the exact Pareto front (verified by enumerating every feasible XI) with a hypervolume ratio of 0.9999. In a backtest on 45 India T20Is played in 2025 or later, the recommended XI shares on average 8.60 of 11 players with the XI actually fielded, against 8.27 for random valid XIs and 9.33 for simply repeating the previous match's XI. We therefore position the method as a decision-support tool that exposes the batting-bowling trade-off, not as a predictor of selectors' choices, and we report the limitations that lead to this conclusion.

**Keywords:** cricket team selection, multi-objective optimisation, NSGA-II, XGBoost, neural network, T20, decision support

## 1. Introduction

Team selection in cricket combines two conflicting goals. A batting-heavy XI reduces the number of specialist bowlers, and a bowling-heavy XI shortens the batting order. Selectors resolve this trade-off with experience, and objective methods are comparatively recent. Ahmed et al. [1] formulated IPL team selection as a constrained bi-objective problem (maximise team batting average, minimise team bowling average) under a budget, a foreign-player limit and role constraints, solved it with a modified NSGA-II, and selected a final team using a knee-point and multi-criteria analysis. Earlier work used single-objective integer programming (Gerber and Sharp; Lourens; Warren; all as cited in [1]).

Two aspects of [1] motivate this work.

1. **Static player quality.** Each player is described by a career batting average and bowling average, so the model cannot react to recent form, and a player with a few matches can look better or worse than he is. The authors themselves add hand-made rules (300 runs to count as a batsman, 20 wickets to count as a bowler, a penalty of 100 for non-bowlers) to guard against this.
2. **No out-of-sample evidence.** The optimised teams are compared with the 2011 Chennai Super Kings team on the same statistics, so the reported superiority is "theoretical" in the authors' words. Nothing shows that the method recovers teams that selectors really fielded.

We keep the bi-objective NSGA-II structure and the knee-point decision rule of [1], and replace the two weak parts: player scores become *learned, form-aware predictions*, and evaluation becomes a *time-split backtest against real XIs*.

**Contributions.**

- A leakage-free feature pipeline over 4,801 ball-by-ball T20 matches, using shrinkage towards a prior so that players with few matches are handled without threshold rules.
- A statistically tested comparison of a rolling-form baseline, Ridge, XGBoost and a neural network for predicting next-match runs and wickets (match-level cluster bootstrap).
- A predict-then-optimise selector: NSGA-II over 11-player subsets, validated against exhaustive enumeration, with knee-point decision making.
- An honest backtest on India T20Is, including negative findings: the previous match's XI is a stronger predictor of the next XI than our method, and opposition and venue features change the chosen XI very little.

## 2. Related Work

Ahmed et al. [1] is the closest work and is summarised in Table 1 together with our differences. Integer-programming approaches to squad selection [as cited in [1]] optimise one weighted objective, which hides the trade-off. Saikia and Bhattacharjee (as cited in [1]) classify IPL all-rounders using strike rate and economy rate, which supports using several conflicting criteria. NSGA-II [2] is the standard elitist multi-objective evolutionary algorithm and knee points as preferred solutions are analysed in [3]. Gradient boosting [4] is a strong default for tabular data, which is why we compare it with a neural network.

**Table 1. Comparison with Ahmed et al. [1].**

| Aspect | Ahmed et al. [1] | This work |
|---|---|---|
| Data | 129 IPL-4 players, international T20 career averages | 5,108 players, 4,801 ball-by-ball matches (international and IPL), 2005 to 2026 |
| Player score | Career batting / bowling average, with threshold rules | Learned prediction of next-match runs and wickets from recent form, shrinkage for small samples |
| Objectives | Team batting average (max), team bowling average (min) | Expected team batting strength (max), expected team bowling strength (max) |
| Constraints | Captain, wicket-keeper, at most 4 foreign players, budget | 11 players, at least 1 wicket-keeper, at least 5 bowling options |
| Representation | Code-vector and variable-vector with role lists | Binary inclusion vector with a repair operator that enforces exactly 11 players |
| Algorithm | NSGA-II, SBX crossover, polynomial mutation, population 400, 400 generations | NSGA-II (pymoo), uniform crossover, bit-flip mutation, population 80, 60 generations |
| Decision making | Knee region, then multi-criteria non-dominated sorting | Knee point (distance from the line joining the extremes) |
| Validation | Comparison with the 2011 CSK team on the same statistics | Time-split backtest on 45 India T20Is against real XIs and four baselines, with bootstrap confidence intervals; optimiser checked against exhaustive enumeration |

## 3. Data

**Source.** Ball-by-ball JSON files from Cricsheet [6]: `t20s_male_json` (3,558 men's international T20 matches) and `ipl_male_json` (1,243 IPL matches). Cricsheet withholds men's Afghanistan matches and the Afghanistan Premier League, so these are absent from the data.

**Parsing rules.** Each match is converted into one row per player who was named in the XI (106,194 rows; 78,285 international and 27,909 IPL; 5,108 distinct players, identified by the Cricsheet registry identifier because names are abbreviated and not unique).

- *Batting:* runs, balls faced (wides excluded), fours, sixes, dot balls, dismissal, batting position (order of arrival at the crease), balls faced in the powerplay (overs 1 to 6) and in the death overs (overs 16 to 20). A player counts as having batted if he reached the crease, which separates a duck from not batting.
- *Bowling:* legal balls, runs conceded (byes and leg-byes excluded), wickets (bowled, caught, caught and bowled, lbw, stumped, hit wicket; run-outs are not credited to the bowler), dot balls, wides, no-balls, powerplay and death balls.
- *Fielding:* catches, stumpings and run-outs from the fielder lists; substitute fielders are ignored.
- *Context:* date, venue, team, opposition, innings, toss and team totals. Super overs are excluded.

A spot check against an independently prepared dataset reproduced the same innings for the same player (100 runs off 47 balls, 7 fours, 8 sixes).

**Split.** All evaluation uses time: training before 2024-01-01, validation in 2024 (used only to tune XGBoost), test from 2025-01-01. For the prediction experiments we keep players with at least 15 T20 appearances (1,856 players); the batting task has 50,498 training-plus-validation and 14,051 test rows, the bowling task 37,352 and 10,188. Runs are modelled only for innings in which the player batted, wickets only for innings in which he bowled.

## 4. Methodology

Figure 1 shows the full pipeline.

![Figure 1. End-to-end pipeline](paper_figs/fig1_pipeline.png)

### 4.1 Leakage-free features

For player *i* and match *t*, every feature is computed from matches strictly before *t* (a one-step shift inside each player's history), so a match never sees its own outcome.

- **Rolling statistics:** means of the batting and bowling quantities over the last 5 matches, the last 15 matches, and the whole career, using only matches in which the player batted (or bowled).
- **Shrinkage:** to stabilise small samples we use

  `mu_hat = (S + K * mu_0) / (N + K)`, with K = 5,

  where S is the sum of the statistic over the window, N the number of qualifying matches and mu_0 the mean over all training data (before 2024). A debutant therefore starts at the population mean and moves towards his own record as N grows; no hand-made thresholds are needed.
- **Usage:** `p_bat_15` and `p_bowl_15`, the share of the last 15 matches in which the player batted or bowled; fielding rates; matches played; days since the previous match.
- **Context:** the venue's mean team total, the team's batting form, and the opposition's runs-conceded and batting form over their last 20 matches (all past-only, shrunk to the global mean).
- **As-of rows:** one artificial row per player dated in the far future gives the features of any player at any date, including players who did not play that match. The selector needs this for squad members who are not in the actual XI.

### 4.2 Prediction models

Two regression targets: runs (given the player batted) and wickets (given he bowled). All models use the same features.

- **Baseline:** the shrunk rolling 15-match mean of the target.
- **Ridge regression** (alpha = 50) on standardised features.
- **XGBoost** [4] with Poisson objective (both targets are non-negative and skewed counts), grid search over depth {2, 3, 4}, learning rate {0.03, 0.08} and minimum child weight {10, 30} with early stopping on the 2024 validation year; final model averages 5 seeds.
- **Neural network (ANN):** a feed-forward network with hidden layers (64, 32), L2 penalty 0.1, early stopping on 15% of the training data, averaged over 5 seeds (scikit-learn [7]).

Evaluation metrics are MAE, RMSE and Spearman rank correlation on the test period. Uncertainty of the difference between two models is estimated with a **match-level cluster bootstrap** (400 resamples of matches, since the players of one match are not independent).

### 4.3 Team objectives

For a squad pool P and a team T ⊂ P with |T| = 11, using predictions for the match being selected:

```
f1(T) = sum over i in T of  p_bat_i  *  E[runs_i | batted]        (maximise)
f2(T) = sum over i in T of  p_bowl_i *  E[wickets_i | bowled]     (maximise)
```

Weighting by usage probability avoids counting the runs of a specialist bowler who rarely bats. Interpreted literally, f1 is the expected runs scored by the XI off the bat and f2 the expected wickets taken by the XI. As in [1], fielding is a secondary criterion and is not optimised here.

Constraints: exactly 11 distinct players; at least one wicket-keeper (career stumpings at least 2, because Cricsheet has no role field); at least five bowling options (`p_bowl_15` at least 0.35).

Toss and innings are excluded from the selection model because a real selector must name the XI before the toss.

### 4.4 NSGA-II and decision making

A team is a binary vector over the pool. The initial population contains random 11-subsets; uniform crossover (probability 0.9) and bit-flip mutation are followed by a **repair** step that adds or removes random players until exactly 11 remain, so the cardinality constraint never fails. The two role constraints are handled as inequality constraints with NSGA-II's constraint-domination rule. Population 80, 60 generations, implemented with pymoo [5].

Because the pool is small (about 15 players, so at most a few hundred thousand teams), we also compute the **exact** Pareto front by enumerating every combination. This gives a ground truth for the optimiser: we report the *hypervolume ratio* (NSGA-II front divided by exact front, reference point at the origin).

From the front we choose the **knee point**: after normalising both objectives to [0, 1], the solution with the largest distance to the straight line joining the two extreme solutions [3].

### 4.5 Backtest protocol

For every India T20I from 2025-01-01 (45 matches):

1. **Pool:** all India players who appeared in an India T20I of the same series or tournament within 45 days of the match (mean pool size 14.7). This is a proxy for the announced squad.
2. Compute each pool player's as-of features and the match context (venue, opposition), predict, form f1 and f2, run NSGA-II, take the knee XI.
3. Compare with the XI actually fielded by the number of common players (0 to 11).

Baselines: (a) the same optimiser using the rolling-form scores instead of the learned predictions; (b) the previous India match's XI (persistence); (c) the eleven most frequently selected players over India's last 10 matches; (d) a random valid XI from the pool. Confidence intervals are bootstrap percentile intervals over matches.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Prediction accuracy (test: 2025 onwards)

![Figure 2. Model comparison](paper_figs/fig2_model_comparison.png)

**Table 2. Batting: predicting runs when the player batted (14,051 test innings).**

| Model | MAE | RMSE | Spearman |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global mean | 15.428 | 20.646 | n/a |
| Rolling-15 baseline | 14.418 | 19.500 | 0.339 |
| Ridge | 13.820 | 19.055 | 0.384 |
| XGBoost | **13.776** | 19.049 | **0.386** |
| ANN (64, 32) | 13.809 | **19.030** | 0.384 |

**Table 3. Bowling: predicting wickets when the player bowled (10,188 test innings).**

| Model | MAE | RMSE | Spearman |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global mean | 0.803 | 1.073 | n/a |
| Rolling-15 baseline | 0.853 | 1.077 | 0.111 |
| Ridge | 0.822 | 1.058 | 0.171 |
| XGBoost | 0.823 | **1.057** | **0.173** |
| ANN (64, 32) | 0.830 | 1.059 | 0.163 |

**Table 4. Improvement over the rolling-form baseline (MAE difference, 95% match-level bootstrap CI).**

| Comparison | Batting | Bowling |
|---|---|---|
| XGBoost minus baseline | [-0.710, -0.574] | [-0.035, -0.027] |
| ANN minus baseline | [-0.683, -0.539] | [-0.028, -0.017] |

Findings. (i) Both learned models improve significantly on the baseline, since the intervals exclude zero. (ii) XGBoost, the neural network and Ridge are within about 0.05 runs of each other, so the signal is largely linear in the engineered features; we do not claim that either XGBoost or the ANN is better. (iii) For wickets, MAE is misleading: a constant prediction of the training mean has a lower MAE (0.803) than any model because counts are dominated by zeros and ones, while RMSE and rank correlation, which are more informative for counts, favour the models. (iv) The largest XGBoost importances for batting are the recent batting-position history and career runs; for bowling they are the career dot-ball count, career wickets and recent bowling usage.

*Preliminary experiment.* On a first dataset of only 34 India players (1,498 innings) no model beat a rolling-form baseline (runs MAE 16.92 for the ANN against 16.85 for the baseline). Enlarging the data to 5,108 players is what produced the significant gains above, which suggests that data volume, not model complexity, is the bottleneck.

### 5.2 Validity of the optimiser

Across the 45 selection problems, the NSGA-II front reaches a mean hypervolume ratio of 0.9999 (minimum 0.9958) relative to the exact front, and the knee XI obtained from NSGA-II and from the exact front share 8.60 vs 8.58 players with the real XI on average. NSGA-II is therefore a reliable solver on this problem size; its advantage over enumeration would appear only for larger pools (for example, all Indian players active in the last 12 months), which we leave to future work.

### 5.3 Backtest against real India XIs

![Figure 5. Overlap with the real XI](paper_figs/fig5_backtest_overlap.png)

**Table 5. Mean overlap with the XI actually fielded (out of 11), 45 India T20Is, 2025 onwards.**

| Method | Mean overlap | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| NSGA-II knee XI (learned scores) | 8.60 | [8.33, 8.89] |
| Exact-front knee XI (learned scores) | 8.58 | [8.27, 8.87] |
| Knee XI with rolling-form scores (no learning) | 8.73 | [8.47, 9.02] |
| Previous match's XI (persistence) | **9.33** | [9.00, 9.67] |
| Top 11 by appearances in last 10 matches | 8.18 | [7.78, 8.58] |
| Random valid XI from the pool | 8.27 | [8.12, 8.43] |

Findings. (i) The learned-score XI is only about 0.3 players above a random valid XI, because the pool is small (about 15 players, from which 11 are chosen), so any method overlaps heavily. (ii) Replacing the learned scores by rolling-form scores does not change the result (paired difference about -0.13 players, CI containing zero): improved *prediction accuracy* did not translate into better *reproduction of selectors' choices*. (iii) Repeating the previous XI is better than every optimisation-based method (paired difference about -0.7 players, CI excluding zero). Real selection is dominated by continuity, fitness, rotation and tactical roles that are not in our data.

In 71% of matches the real XI is dominated by our predicted Pareto front, i.e. some feasible XI has at least as high predicted batting *and* bowling strength. This statement is about *predicted* strength, not about real match outcomes, and should not be read as evidence that the XI we propose would have performed better. For comparison, [1] reports the same kind of statement for CSK. In 91% of matches the real XI satisfies our two role constraints, so the constraints are slightly tighter than real practice.

The pool contained 13 players and 51 feasible XIs, of which 9 are Pareto-optimal (the exact front and the NSGA-II front coincide).

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Players | V Suryavanshi, Y Thakur, Ashok Sharma, Ishan Kishan, Prince Yadav, Suryansh Shedge, S Dube, Tilak Varma, MP Yadav, R Bishnoi, Abhishek Sharma | V Suryavanshi, Y Thakur, Ashok Sharma, Ishan Kishan, Prince Yadav, SS Iyer, S Dube, Tilak Varma, MP Yadav, R Bishnoi, Abhishek Sharma | RK Singh, V Suryavanshi, Ashok Sharma, Ishan Kishan, Prince Yadav, SS Iyer, S Dube, Tilak Varma, MP Yadav, R Bishnoi, Abhishek Sharma | RK Singh, V Suryavanshi, Y Thakur, Ashok Sharma, Ishan Kishan, SS Iyer, Suryansh Shedge, Tilak Varma, MP Yadav, R Bishnoi, Abhishek Sharma |

The knee XI shares 9 of 11 players with the real XI (it selects S Dube and Prince Yadav where the selectors chose RK Singh and Suryansh Shedge). The real XI is dominated by the batting-extreme team of the front (higher f1 and higher f2). Moving from the knee to the batting extreme gains about 13 units of batting strength and loses about 1.25 units of bowling strength, which quantifies the trade-off a selector faces.

**Player frequency on the front.** Following the "frequency of players on the trade-off front" idea of [1], six of the 13 players (V Suryavanshi, Ishan Kishan, Ashok Sharma, S Dube, Prince Yadav, Abhishek Sharma) appear in every Pareto-optimal team and can be regarded as the core of the squad; SS Iyer and Tilak Varma appear in 89% of the teams and RK Singh in 33% (Figure 4). In [1] only 35 of 129 players appeared on the front; with a pool of 13 all players appear at least once.

### 5.5 Sensitivity to opposition and venue

To test whether the chosen XI adapts to conditions, we re-ran the selection for all 45 matches with the opposition and venue features set to their 10th percentile (low-scoring conditions) and to their 90th percentile (high-scoring conditions). On average only **0.36 of 11 players changed**, the XI was identical in 64% of matches, and the rank correlation of player scores across the two settings was 0.999. Opposition and venue currently enter only as team-level quantities common to all players, so they shift every score in the same direction. Player-specific features (a bowler's record against a given opposition, a batsman's record at a ground) are required to make the selector genuinely conditions-aware.

## 6. Discussion and Limitations

- **Pool construction.** The squad proxy uses players who appeared later in the same series, which mildly favours all methods (visible in the 8.27 random baseline). A stricter test would use the officially announced squad, or all players active in the previous 12 months, and there NSGA-II would be needed rather than enumeration.
- **Circularity of usage.** `p_bat_15` and `p_bowl_15` are derived from past selections and batting orders, so the model partly inherits selectors' habits; this makes the objectives realistic but blurs the line between performance and selection.
- **Proxy constraints.** Wicket-keeper status is inferred from stumpings, and "bowling option" from recent bowling share. Cricsheet contains no role, fitness or injury data.
- **Coverage.** Only international and IPL matches are included; Afghanistan matches are withheld by the source; the backtest covers one team and 45 matches.
- **Metric.** Overlap with the real XI measures agreement with selectors, not team quality. Predicted strength is not validated against match results.
- **Objective simplicity.** Two additive objectives ignore batting order interactions, overs distribution among bowlers, fielding, captaincy and the third objective used in [1].
- **Tuning.** Hyperparameters were tuned on 2024 only and fixed for 2025+, which is deliberately conservative but may leave some accuracy unused.

---

## 7. Conclusion and Future Work

We replaced the static career averages of an established NSGA-II team-selection model with learned, form-aware predictions and evaluated it out of sample. The learned predictions are significantly more accurate than a rolling-form baseline, the optimiser matches the exact Pareto front, and the framework exposes the batting-bowling trade-off in a form usable by a selector (front, knee XI, player frequencies). The backtest, however, shows that real XIs are better reproduced by continuity than by optimisation, and that opposition and venue currently have almost no influence. We therefore present the system as decision support.

Future work: (1) a third objective or constraint given by a learned *probability of selection* trained on past India XIs, combining performance with selector behaviour; (2) player-versus-opposition and player-at-venue features with shrinkage; (3) larger pools and more teams (all IPL franchises) where evolutionary search is necessary; (4) ODI and Test formats with format-specific models, since performance does not transfer across formats [1]; (5) validation against match outcomes.

## Appendix A. Reproducibility

**Software.** Python 3, pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, XGBoost, pymoo. Scripts: `parse.py` (JSON to player-match table), `feat3.py` (features and as-of rows), `model2.py` (model comparison and bootstrap), `selection.py` (final models, NSGA-II, exact front, backtest).

**Sizes.** Player-match table 106,194 x 39; with features and 5,108 as-of rows 111,302 x 118; modelling subset (players with at least 15 appearances) 88,490 rows.

**Hyperparameters.**

| Component | Setting |
|---|---|
| Shrinkage | K = 5, prior = training mean before 2024 |
| Rolling windows | last 5, last 15, career |
| XGBoost (batting, model comparison) | Poisson, depth 4, learning rate 0.03, min child weight 30, 351 trees, subsample 0.8, column sample 0.7, lambda 5 |
| XGBoost (bowling, model comparison) | Poisson, depth 2, learning rate 0.03, min child weight 30, 652 trees, same sampling |
| XGBoost for selection | trained on all players before 2025, no toss/innings features, 3 seeds (386 trees batting, 717 trees bowling) |
| ANN | MLP (64, 32), alpha 0.1, early stopping (15% validation, patience 15), up to 400 iterations, 5 seeds |
| Ridge | alpha 50 |
| NSGA-II | population 80, 60 generations, uniform crossover 0.9, bit-flip mutation, repair to 11 players, duplicates eliminated |
